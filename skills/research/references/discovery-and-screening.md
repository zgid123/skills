# Discovery and screening

## Define a proportionate search

Establish the question and key concepts from the supplied context. For focused discovery, use the core concepts and synonyms. For a systematic or scoping review, establish the review question, eligibility criteria, databases, screening process, and extraction plan before searching; choose a discipline-appropriate framework rather than imposing a clinical framework on every field.

Inspect existing search logs and screening decisions first. Reuse records whose scope, provenance, and version are adequate; search for updates or unresolved needs rather than automatically repeating completed searches. Define completion in terms of the requested answer and evidence coverage. A focused shortlist may be complete once its requested scope is supported and relevant conflicting evidence has been considered; a formal review must complete its protocol even when initial findings seem sufficient. A time or access limit should produce an explicit shortfall, not a claim of completeness.

Use available scholarly indexes, publisher search, repositories, and general web search as appropriate to the discipline. Verify current interfaces and actual coverage when using them; do not claim to have queried a database through a search-engine result mentioning it. When browsing is unavailable, deliver a proposed strategy or screen supplied material and state that no live search occurred.

## Search and verify

1. Build queries from concept groups, synonyms, and meaningful constraints. Use database-supported syntax. Search exclusions can reduce noise but do not replace venue screening.
2. Search more than one appropriate source for a broad review when tools permit. For a narrow task, scale effort to the request. Inspect candidate primary records and follow backward references or forward citations when useful and available.
3. Record the search date, actual source searched, exact query, filters, and coverage limits. Label counts accurately: returned results, inspected records, and included papers are different quantities. Do not invent database totals.
4. Use available titles, abstracts, and metadata to reject clearly irrelevant records and identify duplicates before retrieving full text or resolving every bibliographic field. Record clear venue exclusions immediately. Deduplicate DOI matches and verified versions of the same work; a similar title alone is insufficient. Retain version relationships and do not count versions as independent studies.
5. For retained candidates, verify title, authors, year, venue, publication status, DOI or stable URL, and available text. Do not manufacture missing metadata. Apply [Source policy](source-policy.md) before admitting evidence. Cite the version actually supporting the claim. Keep uncertain eligibility or identity pending rather than rejecting or accepting it on a guess.
6. Screen retained candidates against the remaining relevance and eligibility criteria, reading beyond the abstract when needed. Keep accepted, excluded, and pending decisions distinct, with reasons and evidence access. If the request asks for more eligible papers than verified results provide, report the shortfall and continue or propose a wider search rather than filling the list with weak or excluded sources.

After the first useful evidence, form a provisional synthesis and identify the next search's purpose: resolve a missing claim, explain a contradiction, test a gap, or find plausible counterevidence. Include searches that could challenge the emerging answer. Stop focused discovery when its completion criteria are met; do not expand into tangential populations or outcomes without a reason tied to the question. In a formal review, use emerging findings to inform analysis while retaining the agreed protocol; document any justified amendments.

## Useful deliverables

For a reading list, give each paper's verified citation or stable link, relevance, publication status, text access, and reading priority. Explain priority through relevance and evidential usefulness rather than prestige alone.

For learning-oriented discovery, translate the user's topic into concept groups, synonyms, and useful search terms. Arrange retained papers into a reading path that identifies what each teaches and any prerequisites: an orienting review or tutorial when useful, then relevant foundational and subsequent primary work. Do not imply that the oldest paper must be read first or that the most cited paper is best for a beginner. Use backward references and forward citations to explore specific conceptual relationships when available, verifying those relationships in the material inspected. Follow [Learning and concept exploration](learning-and-concept-exploration.md) for the explanation and next learning step. Keep the search record compact in chat unless a detailed audit or formal review is requested.

For a review, maintain a compact search log and screening table:

| Paper ID and title | Year and venue | Publication status | Evidence access | Decision and reason |
| ------------------ | -------------- | ------------------ | --------------- | ------------------- |

Use additional fields only when needed for the project. Separate the accepted reading list from the screening table so excluded items cannot be mistaken for recommendations.

Describe coverage in bounded terms such as “among the sources searched.” A shortlist is not an exhaustive literature review. If conducting a formal review, retain reproducible screening counts and a flow summary where appropriate; do not claim independent dual screening or registration unless they occurred.
