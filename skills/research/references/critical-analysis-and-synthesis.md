# Critical analysis and synthesis

## Reading a paper

Apply [Source policy](source-policy.md) first. Choose reading depth by the paper's role in the requested answer:

1. Screen titles and abstracts for relevance before investing in detailed reading. Clearly irrelevant papers need only a screening reason.
2. For retained papers, inspect the sections supporting the intended claims, including relevant figures, tables, appendices, and corrections. An abstract can support a discovery decision or bounded abstract summary, not an uninspected appraisal of methods.
3. For papers central to the argument, disputed findings, or a requested critical appraisal, examine methods and results in depth with the surrounding context needed to judge them. Follow a formal review's extraction and appraisal requirements even when a faster reading would suffice for a shortlist.

Reuse traceable reading notes for claims they already support; inspect additional sections when a new claim, changed version, or unresolved contradiction requires them. State the material actually inspected, including when relying on prior notes. If only selected sections or the abstract are available, bound the analysis explicitly. Missing information in an abstract is not proof that the full study omitted it.

For understanding-focused reading, use these depths as successive passes: establish the big picture, understand the mechanism and supporting evidence, then reconstruct technical details only where needed for the user's goal. Explain central figures or equations when necessary rather than forcing a linear read of every page. Adapt the sequence to the discipline and paper type. Follow [Learning and concept exploration](learning-and-concept-exploration.md) for prerequisites, layered explanations, worked examples, maps, and optional understanding checks; preserve the evidence and access boundaries at every depth.

Use a reading note scaled to the question:

- **Identity and access:** verified citation or paper ID, publication status, version, and sections inspected.
- **Question and contribution:** the problem, motivating gap, and contribution claimed by the authors.
- **Approach:** study design, theory or mechanism, participants or data, assumptions, comparison conditions, measures, and analysis as available.
- **Findings:** outcomes and reported uncertainty with units, denominators, conditions, and supporting section, table, figure, or page when available. Distinguish the authors' interpretation from the actual result.
- **Appraisal:** strengths and weaknesses of the design, evidence supporting the claims, alternative explanations, and limits to generalization.
- **Relevance:** how the paper informs the user's question and what remains unanswered.

Match appraisal to the method. For quantitative work consider sampling, confounding, baselines, leakage, measurement, uncertainty, and robustness. For qualitative work consider recruitment, context, reflexivity, analytic transparency, and evidence for themes. For theoretical or conceptual work assess assumptions, logical support, scope, and counterexamples. Do not grade every discipline by machine-learning benchmarks or randomized trials.

For short summaries, preserve the essential question, approach, findings, limitations, and access boundary without forcing every heading. Do not reproduce long copyrighted passages; paraphrase faithfully and quote only short, necessary excerpts with verified locations.

## Comparing and synthesizing

Build an evidence matrix for multiple papers, using only fields relevant to the question:

| Paper ID | Question and setting | Design and data | Main finding | Limitations | Relevance and access |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Start the matrix and a provisional synthesis as soon as useful evidence is available, then update them as the corpus develops. Use unsupported claims, conflicting findings, and plausible alternative explanations to prioritize the next reading or search, including evidence that could overturn the provisional answer. Early synthesis guides attention; it does not establish consensus or replace a formal review's protocol.

Group findings by theme, mechanism, method, or competing explanation. Explain agreement and disagreement through study conditions and evidence quality. Keep null and negative findings visible. Do not tally conclusions as votes or average incomparable metrics, populations, or tasks. A meta-analysis requires justified comparability and an explicit statistical method. Data Analyst is optional for computation; otherwise use available tools, document and verify the analysis, or disclose why computation could not be performed.

Tie each substantive synthesis statement to the papers supporting it. Distinguish a reported limitation from your own critique. Keep uncertainty visible where the available text cannot resolve an apparent contradiction. Reviews that cite the same primary study do not supply independent replications.

## Research gaps

Express a gap as a bounded evidence problem: what is unresolved, where the literature establishes it, why resolving it matters, and what study could test it. Distinguish an understudied setting from a missing method, inconsistent evidence, inadequate evaluation, or a conceptual inconsistency.

Treat gaps and novelty as provisional under the documented search coverage. “Not found in this search” does not establish “no prior work exists.” Check promising gaps through a targeted follow-up search when tools permit. Avoid inventing a gap merely because the user needs a contribution or describing excluded IEEE Access work as nonexistent.
